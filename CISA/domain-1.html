CISA Domain 1: Information System Auditing Process

The audit charter is the foundational document for the IS audit function. It formally grants authority and defines the purpose, scope, and responsibilities of auditors. Without a valid charter, audits lack legitimacy.

  • Authority: Provides organizational independence and access to information
  • Responsibility: Clarifies auditor roles, expectations, and boundaries
  • Approval: Must be approved by senior management or board-level governance
  • Review: Should be periodically reviewed and updated

Real-world tip: Auditors should always validate that a charter exists and is current before starting an audit.

ISACA's IT Assurance Framework (ITAF) provides the professional standards and guidelines for IS auditing. These are essential for ensuring audits are consistent, reliable, and defendable.

Structure of ITAF:

  • General Standards: Ethics, objectivity, due care, proficiency
  • Performance Standards: Risk-based planning, supervision, evidence collection
  • Reporting Standards: Accuracy, completeness, clarity, and timeliness

Real-world insight: Many audit reports fail not due to technical flaws, but because they do not align with professional reporting standards.

IS auditors are held to a high ethical standard, defined by ISACA's Code of Ethics. These principles are non-negotiable and guide professional conduct.

  • Serve stakeholders lawfully, honestly, and diligently
  • Maintain and improve competence through continuing education
  • Provide accurate, fair, and objective information
  • Protect confidential information
  • Comply with relevant laws and regulations

Exam tip: Ethics are always tested in scenario-based questions. Understand not just what they say, but how they’re applied.

Audit planning is critical for effectiveness and efficiency. It determines what will be audited, how, by whom, and when.

Steps in Audit Planning:

  1. Understand business environment and processes
  2. Determine audit scope and objectives
  3. Perform risk assessment and prioritize areas
  4. Define resources, timeframes, and tools
  5. Communicate plan with auditees and management

Pro tip: Always ensure that audit scope aligns with risk exposure and organizational priorities.

Risk-based audit planning ensures that audits focus on areas with the highest potential impact or likelihood of failure.

  • Uses risk registers, past audit results, and business context
  • Prioritizes systems that affect confidentiality, integrity, or availability (CIA)
  • Considers compliance, financial, and operational risks

Real-world application: Auditing high-risk areas first increases audit value and reduces the likelihood of missed critical findings.

The audit objectives define what the audit seeks to achieve. Objectives should be clear, measurable, and aligned with business and audit risk priorities.

  • Assess the design and operating effectiveness of controls
  • Ensure compliance with laws, regulations, and internal policies
  • Evaluate risk management and governance practices

Example: For a payroll system audit, objectives may include validating data integrity, access controls, and regulatory compliance.

Audit evidence forms the foundation of audit findings. It must be sufficient, relevant, reliable, and timely.

  • Can be physical, documentary, electronic, or testimonial
  • Collected through observation, interviews, system walkthroughs, and control testing
  • Should be retained for post-audit verification and defense

ISACA Tip: Use triangulation — collect evidence from multiple sources to confirm facts.

Audit documentation supports conclusions, provides a trail of work performed, and is essential for quality assurance and future audits.

  • Includes audit plans, working papers, test results, findings, and correspondence
  • Must be clear, complete, and well-organized
  • Should allow another auditor to re-perform the audit and reach the same conclusion

The audit universe is a complete list of auditable entities in the organization, such as processes, systems, departments, and third-party relationships.

  • Used for long-term audit planning and coverage analysis
  • Often visualized in a risk heat map or audit schedule
  • Should be updated periodically based on business and risk changes

Each audit type has unique objectives, stakeholders, and methods. Understanding these is critical for exam success and real-world application.

  • IS Audit: Focuses on IT systems and controls
  • Compliance Audit: Checks adherence to laws, regulations, or internal policies
  • Financial Audit: Examines financial statements and accounting practices
  • Operational Audit: Evaluates efficiency, effectiveness, and economy
  • Forensic Audit: Investigates fraud or suspected misconduct
  • Integrated Audit: Combines IS, operational, and financial auditing
  • Third-party Audit: Evaluates service providers (e.g., SOC reports)

CAATs help auditors automate and enhance audit procedures, increasing efficiency and scope.

  • Include data analysis tools (Excel, ACL, IDEA)
  • Used to test controls, extract anomalies, and identify patterns
  • Can automate sampling, filtering, and exception reporting
  • Critical for auditing large or complex IT environments

Examples: Extracting duplicate transactions, analyzing login attempts, verifying user access rights.

Audit resource management ensures that the right people, tools, and training are in place to meet audit objectives.

  • Staff should possess relevant skills (e.g., technical, analytical, communication)
  • Ongoing training must address emerging risks like cloud, cybersecurity, and AI
  • Resource planning must align with the audit calendar and business priorities

In complex audits, organizations may outsource audit tasks or use subject matter experts (SMEs) to support specific areas.

  • SMEs may include forensic analysts, penetration testers, or data scientists
  • Auditors must supervise all external work to ensure quality and compliance
  • Engagement contracts must define roles, deliverables, and confidentiality terms

Flashcard Review

Q: What is the difference between audit evidence and audit documentation?

A: Audit evidence supports findings; audit documentation records the audit work and process.

Proper planning and supervision ensure that audit engagements are well-executed and meet professional standards.

  • Engagement plan includes scope, methodology, roles, and timeline
  • Supervision ensures quality control and supports less experienced auditors
  • Includes review of workpapers, discussions, and timely escalations

Effective communication ensures that audit findings are understood, acted upon, and respected.

  • Use clear, concise, and objective language
  • Include issue, impact, root cause, and recommendation
  • Draft reports reviewed internally before finalizing
  • Communication should be timely and tailored to the audience

Reporting Types: Formal reports, management letters, executive summaries

A Quality Assurance & Improvement Program (QAIP) ensures continuous improvement in audit performance.

  • Includes internal and external assessments
  • Reviews audit processes, tools, training, and compliance
  • Results feed back into resource planning and methodology updates

Best Practice: QAIP should align with ISACA and IIA standards and be performed at least every 5 years.

IT governance ensures that IT supports business goals while managing risks and resources responsibly.

  • IS auditors evaluate governance practices like COBIT, ITIL, ISO/IEC 38500
  • Includes roles, responsibilities, accountability, and decision-making structures
  • Effective governance helps prioritize investments and align IT with enterprise strategy

Real-World Tip: IT governance maturity is a common audit focus area.

Flashcard Summary

Q: What are the key components of a high-quality IS audit?

A: Clear audit charter, standards-based execution, effective planning, strong evidence and documentation, skilled auditors, and useful reporting.
